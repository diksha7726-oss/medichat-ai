DIABETES MELLITUS

Overview
Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by high levels of glucose in the blood. It occurs when the pancreas does not produce sufficient insulin or when the body cannot effectively use the insulin produced. Insulin is essential for transporting glucose from the bloodstream into cells where it is used for energy.

Human Physiology Background
The pancreas is a gland located behind the stomach. It contains beta cells that produce insulin. Insulin allows glucose from food to enter cells for energy production. When insulin production is inadequate or when the body develops resistance to insulin, glucose accumulates in the blood, leading to hyperglycemia.

Types of Diabetes
Type 1 Diabetes
Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disorder in which the immune system attacks insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. As a result, insulin production becomes minimal or absent. Individuals with type 1 diabetes require lifelong insulin therapy.

Type 2 Diabetes
Type 2 diabetes is the most common form of diabetes. It occurs when the body's cells become resistant to insulin or when insulin production becomes insufficient. It is strongly associated with obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and genetic predisposition.

Gestational Diabetes
Gestational diabetes occurs during pregnancy when hormonal changes interfere with insulin function. Although it often resolves after childbirth, it increases the risk of developing type 2 diabetes later in life.

Prediabetes
Prediabetes is a condition where blood glucose levels are higher than normal but not yet high enough to be classified as diabetes.

Symptoms
Common symptoms include:
Frequent urination
Excessive thirst
Increased hunger
Unexplained weight loss
Fatigue
Blurred vision
Slow healing wounds
Frequent infections

Risk Factors
Family history of diabetes
Obesity
Sedentary lifestyle
Poor diet
High blood pressure
High cholesterol
Age over 45

Diagnosis
Doctors diagnose diabetes through laboratory tests such as:
Fasting plasma glucose test
Oral glucose tolerance test
HbA1c test
Random blood glucose test

Treatment
Treatment strategies include:
Insulin therapy
Oral medications such as metformin
Healthy diet
Regular physical activity
Blood sugar monitoring

Complications
Long-term complications include:
Heart disease
Kidney disease
Nerve damage
Eye damage
Foot ulcers

Prevention
Maintaining healthy weight
Regular physical exercise
Balanced diet
Limiting sugar intake
Regular medical screening
